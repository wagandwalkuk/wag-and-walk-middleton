Behind The Lead · Starting From Zero

Starting A Business With A Mobile Phone, Rover, And A Love Of Dogs

How the first customer, early mistakes and the need to solve real problems turned a phone-based side project into an operating local business.

Published 24 June 2026 by Nathan Lothian

In short: Wag & Walk Middleton did not begin with investment, specialist software or a polished plan. It began with a phone, a Rover account and the belief that a useful local service could be built by solving one genuine problem at a time.

When people look at Wag & Walk Middleton today, they see a brand.

They see a website, social media pages, reviews, uniforms, policies, onboarding processes, local SEO pages, scheduling systems and a growing customer base.

What they don’t see is how it started.

Because the truth is that Wag & Walk Middleton didn’t begin with a business plan, investment funding, specialist software or years of industry experience.

It started with a mobile phone, a Rover account and a love of dogs.

That’s it.

What “Starting From Zero” Actually Looked Like

People often say they started from zero, but most of the time they didn’t.

They had contacts. They had experience. They had customers waiting. They had money set aside. They had equipment.

When I started Wag & Walk Middleton in March 2026, I genuinely had none of those things.

I had:

  • Zero clients.
  • Zero revenue.
  • Zero social media followers.
  • No website.
  • No logo.
  • No business bank account.
  • No insurance.
  • No uniform.
  • No reviews.
  • No dog walking qualifications.
  • No vehicle.
  • No systems.

I didn’t even have a laptop.

I had a mobile phone and a newly created Rover account.

My first business investment was a £20 verification fee. That was my entire startup cost.

At the time, I didn’t realise how important that would become.

Because when people think about starting a business, they often focus on what they don’t have. More money. More qualifications. More experience. More certainty. More equipment.

I didn’t have any of that. What I did have was an idea I believed could work.

The Longest Two Weeks

After paying the Rover fee, I expected enquiries to start arriving. Not hundreds. Just one or two.

Instead, nothing happened. For two weeks.

No messages. No enquiries. No bookings. No customers.

I found myself checking Rover constantly. Every day that passed created a new question.

Were my prices wrong? Did people not trust me? Were the photos bad? Was it because I had no reviews? Had I completely overestimated the opportunity?

At the same time, I started looking at project management jobs again. The same jobs I’d deliberately stepped away from. The same jobs that had filled me with dread only weeks earlier.

Looking back now, I think this is where a lot of people quit. Not because the idea is bad, but because reality doesn’t immediately validate it.

Starting a business is often just a battle against your own doubts until the market gives you an answer.

Eventually, I got mine. His name was Rudy.

The Customer Who Changed Everything

Rudy was my first booking.

Looking back, it’s difficult to overstate how important that moment was.

The obvious thing was that somebody had trusted me with their dog. That mattered.

But the bigger thing was what the booking represented. Proof.

Proof that somebody needed the service. Proof that somebody was willing to pay. Proof that this wasn’t just an idea anymore.

Rudy’s owner worked a changing shift pattern and needed ongoing support. This wasn’t a one-off booking. It was recurring business.

For the first time, I could start seeing how the numbers worked.

I remember realising that one customer had effectively covered my mortgage payment.

That wasn’t enough to make me rich. It wasn’t enough to make me successful. But it was enough to make me believe.

And belief changes everything.

Making It Up As I Went Along

People often assume new businesses start with polished systems and processes.

Mine absolutely didn’t.

When it was just Rudy, the business was held together with WhatsApp. That was the system.

Customer communication? WhatsApp. Booking management? WhatsApp. Invoices? Bank transfer. Customer records? My memory.

Meet and greets? Completely improvised. Key management? Rudy’s key went straight onto my personal keyring.

I walked dogs in normal clothes. I had no onboarding process. No checklist. No customer agreements. No policies. No tracking. No reporting. No business software.

If you’d looked behind the curtain, you would’ve realised there wasn’t really a curtain.

I was figuring it out as I went.

My First Business Mistake

Then I made one of my first major mistakes.

During Rudy’s meet and greet, his owner explained that Rover didn’t work particularly well for his changing shift pattern.

I understood exactly what he meant. I also realised that if I had my own insurance, we could manage everything directly.

No Rover fees. More flexibility. Better for everyone.

So that’s what I did. I got insured and moved him off-platform.

It seemed like a great decision. Then Rover banned me for seven days.

At the time, it felt disastrous. But the ban ended up becoming one of the most important moments in the business.

Because it forced me to stop relying on somebody else’s platform. If Rover wasn’t going to be my lead source, I needed my own.

The second mistake happened around the same time. I’d accidentally quoted Rudy my 30-minute rate for 60-minute walks.

For two weeks I earned £11 per hour. Less than minimum wage.

I had to have an awkward conversation and explain the mistake. Fortunately, Rudy’s owner was incredibly understanding.

The lesson was simple. Pricing isn’t just a number. It’s a system. Get it wrong and everything becomes harder.

The Rover Ban That Built The Business

The Rover ban forced me to think differently.

Instead of asking, “How do I get more Rover bookings?” I started asking, “How do I build a business?”

That’s when things changed.

I created a brand. I designed a logo. I developed brand guidelines. I created social media accounts. I ordered a uniform. I set up business listings. I explored new lead generation channels.

I started thinking about trust. Not dog walking. Trust.

Because if somebody is handing you their dog and often the keys to their home, trust becomes the entire business.

The interesting thing is that I wasn’t approaching this like a dog walker. I was approaching it like a project manager.

I kept asking myself: “If this was a client at a digital agency, what would we recommend they do?”

That mindset shaped almost everything that followed.

Running A Business From A Mobile Phone

One thing that surprises people is how much of Wag & Walk Middleton was built from a phone.

When I say everything, I mean everything.

I was taking enquiries on WhatsApp. Managing bookings. Creating Instagram content. Taking dog photos. Responding to leads. Managing my calendar. Checking banking. Creating graphics. Running social media.

Everything happened on my phone.

I found myself sitting in cafés between bookings handling admin. A coffee, a phone and a growing list of things to do.

That was effectively my office.

The business grew enough during the first month that I eventually bought a laptop.

Not because I’d planned to. Because I’d reached the point where the business justified it.

That’s an important distinction. I wasn’t buying equipment in preparation for success. I was buying equipment because success was creating a need for it.

The Second Client Problem

One of the biggest surprises in business is that every solution creates a new problem.

Getting the first client was hard. Getting the second client created a completely different challenge.

The second client also worked changing shifts. Suddenly I couldn’t keep everything in my head anymore.

The first client could live in my memory. The second couldn’t.

That’s when the first real system appeared. A calendar.

Then came a meet and greet checklist.

The interesting thing is that I didn’t create the checklist to save time. I created it because I lacked confidence.

Every meet and greet ended the same way. Had I asked the right questions? Had I forgotten something important? Did I have enough information? Had I done a good job?

The checklist removed uncertainty. For the first time, I didn’t have to rely on memory. I could rely on process.

Discovering What The Business Really Was

One of the biggest lessons I’ve learned is that dog walking isn’t really a dog walking business.

It’s a logistics business. Especially when you don’t have a car.

Because every booking wasn’t just about the walk itself. It was about travel. Timing. Capacity. Contingency.

I travel by bus and tram. That means delays happen. Connections matter. Travel time matters.

The moment I started taking on multiple shift-worker clients, I realised fixed appointment times could become a problem.

If one booking moved, the whole day could move. If transport was delayed, multiple customers could be affected. The fear of double-booking was very real.

That’s when I started thinking more deeply about operations. Scheduling. Travel blocks. Booking windows. Capacity management.

What began as dog walking slowly evolved into operational problem solving.

And I loved it.

The Unexpected Transfer Of Skills

One thing I never expected was how useful my previous career would become.

When I left corporate life, I thought I was walking away from project management.

What actually happened was that I finally started using the parts I enjoyed.

Process design. Customer journeys. Branding. Marketing. Capacity planning. Systems thinking. Problem solving.

For years, those skills had felt disconnected from outcomes. Now every improvement produced a visible result.

A better process saved time. A better onboarding experience improved confidence. A better website generated enquiries. A better schedule created capacity.

Everything had a direct impact.

And for the first time, it all clicked.

What Starting From Zero Taught Me

Today, Wag & Walk Middleton looks very different.

The systems exist. The processes exist. The brand exists. The customers exist. The business exists.

But none of those things existed when I started.

The biggest lesson I’ve learned is that businesses don’t start fully formed. They evolve.

The first version of Wag & Walk Middleton was one customer, one WhatsApp chat and one key on a personal keyring.

That’s it. Everything else came later.

If there’s one thing I’d want somebody considering their own business to understand, it’s this:

You do not need everything figured out before you start.

You need an idea you believe in. You need to commit to it. And you need to treat it like a real business.

Because if you treat it like a hobby, it’ll probably stay a hobby.

The confidence doesn’t come first. The systems don’t come first. The certainty doesn’t come first.

Those things are built along the way.

And if I could speak to myself sitting there in March 2026 with nothing but a phone and a Rover account, I’d say:

“You’ve learnt so much over the last 12 years and it’s all going to come in handy. Believe in yourself and you will make it work.”

Turns out that was true.
