How to Decide if It’s Safe to Walk Your Dog in Hot Weather

A practical heat risk assessment guide for dog owners who want to make sensible, evidence-led decisions rather than relying on social media slogans.

Published 22 June 2026 · Updated 10 July 2026 by Wag & Walk Middleton

In short: Hot-weather dog walking is not about one magic temperature. A safer decision considers the forecast, ground temperature, route, shade, water, time of day and the individual dog in front of you.

Every summer, social media fills with videos of people confronting dog owners for walking their dogs in hot weather.

The concern usually comes from a good place. Heatstroke is a genuine emergency and every owner should take it seriously. The problem is that nobody watching those videos knows the full story.

Was the dog out for an hour, or just five minutes to use the toilet? Had the owner chosen a fully shaded woodland route? Had they checked the ground temperature beforehand? Were they carrying fresh drinking water? Does the dog even have access to a secure garden?

Without that context, it is impossible to know whether the owner made a responsible decision or a dangerous one.

Unfortunately, many headlines and social media posts reduce the issue to a single temperature or blanket advice to avoid walking altogether. While these warnings help raise awareness, they rarely explain the factors that actually determine whether a walk is safe.

The reality is that keeping dogs safe in hot weather is not about following one temperature threshold. It is about assessing the conditions, understanding the individual dog and making informed decisions based on evidence rather than assumptions.

At Wag & Walk Middleton, every walk during warm weather begins with a heat risk assessment. Whether you are walking your own dog or looking for a professional solo dog walking service, here is the same process you can follow yourself.

Think your dog may have heatstroke? If your dog is showing signs such as excessive panting, collapse, vomiting, confusion or difficulty standing, treat it as a veterinary emergency. Begin cooling them immediately with cool tap water, move them somewhere cool and contact your vet straight away. This guide is about prevention. For detailed first aid advice, read the guidance from the PDSA and the RSPCA.

Step 1: Start With the Weather Forecast, Not Just the Temperature

The first thing most people look at is the maximum temperature for the day. While that is useful, it only tells part of the story.

Before heading out, consider:

  • Air temperature
  • Humidity
  • UV Index
  • Cloud cover
  • Wind speed
  • The time of day

Two days with the same air temperature can feel completely different. A breezy, cloudy morning may be manageable, while a still, humid afternoon could present a much higher risk.

For local planning, check an hour-by-hour forecast from a reliable source such as the Met Office weather forecast. This first step helps you decide whether additional precautions are needed before you even think about putting your dog’s lead on.

Step 2: Measure the Ground, Not Just the Air

This is one of the biggest misconceptions about walking dogs in hot weather. The temperature shown in your weather app is the air temperature. It is not the temperature of the ground your dog will actually be walking on.

On sunny days, pavements, tarmac and artificial grass absorb heat from the sun and can become significantly hotter than the surrounding air. At the same time, shaded woodland paths, grass and tree-lined routes may remain considerably cooler.

At Wag & Walk Middleton, we use an infrared thermometer whenever conditions warrant it. Rather than assuming the ground is safe, we measure the actual surfaces a dog will be walking on.

That includes checking:

  • Pavements
  • Tarmac
  • Grass
  • Areas in direct sunlight
  • Areas in shade

We also consider how those surfaces are likely to change throughout the walk. A route that is safe at 8:30am may not be safe an hour later. Ground temperature is just one part of the assessment, but it is one of the most important because it is the surface your dog’s paws are actually touching.

Step 3: Every Dog Responds to Heat Differently

Perhaps the biggest mistake people make is assuming every dog has the same level of risk. They do not.

When assessing whether a walk is appropriate, consider:

  • Age
  • Weight
  • Breed
  • Coat type
  • General fitness
  • Medical conditions
  • Previous sensitivity to heat

For example, an elderly dog with a thick black double coat will often need a very different plan from a young, athletic dog with a short coat. Likewise, some dogs know when they are getting tired and naturally slow themselves down. Others will happily keep running until they are dangerously overheated.

This is why blanket rules rarely work. The safest decision depends on the individual dog in front of you. If you are thinking about overall exercise needs, our guide to how much dog walking your dog needs explains why age, breed and personality matter on normal-weather days too.

Step 4: Assess the Route

Even if the weather looks suitable, the route itself can completely change the level of risk.

Ask yourself:

  • Is there plenty of shade?
  • Will most of the walk be on grass?
  • Are there long stretches of exposed pavement?
  • Can you shorten the walk if needed?
  • Is there somewhere to stop and cool down?

Always carry fresh drinking water on every walk during warm weather, even if it is only a short toilet break. Your dog may not normally drink while out, but having water available means you can offer it if they need it.

Choosing a shaded woodland trail instead of an exposed housing estate can dramatically reduce your dog’s heat exposure. Sometimes changing the route is all that is needed to make a walk significantly safer. This is one reason local knowledge matters when choosing a dog walker in Middleton.

Step 5: Not Every Dog Needs the Same Solution

One of the biggest misconceptions is that the only options are to go for a full walk or not go out at all.

In reality, there are several options depending on the conditions. Some dogs have secure gardens and can comfortably wait until the evening. Some need nothing more than a five-minute toilet break before returning indoors. Some do not have access to a garden at all. Others simply refuse to toilet in their garden, making a short outing important for their welfare.

On warmer days, we often replace a full walk with a short toilet break, some time for sniffing and enrichment, before returning home. The goal is not to complete a certain amount of exercise. The goal is to safely meet the individual dog’s needs.

For some dogs, a pet visit or house visit may be more appropriate than a full walk during high-risk weather. If your schedule changes during warm spells, Flex Credits can also help you book support around cooler parts of the day, subject to availability.

Watch for Signs of Heat Stress

Even after carrying out a risk assessment, it is important to keep monitoring your dog throughout the walk.

Stop immediately if you notice:

  • Heavy or excessive panting
  • Thick drool
  • Slowing down unexpectedly
  • Seeking shade
  • Bright red gums
  • Disorientation
  • Reluctance to continue

Move somewhere cool, offer fresh drinking water if they are willing to drink, and allow them to rest. If symptoms become severe or do not improve quickly, seek veterinary attention immediately.

How Wag & Walk Middleton Makes These Decisions

Every warm-weather booking is individually assessed. Rather than relying on a blanket temperature rule, we consider:

  • The latest weather forecast
  • Air temperature
  • Ground surface temperatures
  • Shade along the planned route
  • Surface types
  • Your dog’s age, breed, weight and coat type
  • Medical history and fitness level
  • Access to a secure garden

If conditions are suitable, a normal walk may go ahead. If they are not, we may shorten the walk, switch to a toilet break or recommend a home visit instead. Every decision is based on what is safest for that individual dog while minimising disruption for the owner wherever possible.

That is why you will not see us making blanket cancellations simply because the forecast reaches a certain number. It is also why our weather policy focuses on welfare-led decisions rather than fixed promises to complete a set distance.

Why Context Matters

If you ever see someone walking their dog during hot weather, remember that you do not know everything that has gone into that decision.

It might be a five-minute toilet break. The owner may have checked the route, measured the ground temperature, packed fresh drinking water and chosen the coolest part of the day.

Equally, they may have made the wrong decision. The point is that a few seconds of video or a passing glance rarely tells the whole story.

Responsible dog ownership is not about following internet slogans or judging others from a distance. It is about understanding the risks, assessing the evidence and making informed decisions that put your dog’s welfare first.

Final Thoughts

Hot weather does not automatically mean every walk should be cancelled. Nor does it mean every walk should go ahead. The safest approach sits somewhere in the middle.

By considering the weather, measuring the ground beneath your dog’s paws, carrying fresh drinking water on every walk, assessing the route and understanding your dog’s individual needs, you can make far better decisions than relying on a single temperature or headline.

That is the approach we take at Wag & Walk Middleton because no two dogs are the same, and their care should not be either.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What temperature is too hot to walk a dog?

There is no single safe figure for every dog. Air temperature, humidity, UV, ground temperature, shade, breed, age, weight, health and fitness all affect the risk.

Should I cancel every dog walk in hot weather?

Not always. Some dogs may only need a short toilet break, shaded sniffing time or a home visit instead of a full walk. The safest decision depends on the conditions and the individual dog.

How do I check if the ground is too hot for my dog?

Check the actual surface your dog will walk on, including pavement, tarmac, grass and shaded areas. An infrared thermometer can help, and hand tests can give a rough indication, but ground safety is only one part of the overall heat assessment.

What should I do if my dog shows signs of heatstroke?

Treat suspected heatstroke as an emergency. Move your dog somewhere cool, begin cooling them with cool tap water and contact a vet immediately.
